Great question — this is one of the most commonly misunderstood probe concepts in Kubernetes. The answer is that `initialDelaySeconds` and `startupProbe` solve fundamentally different problems, and using `initialDelaySeconds` alone has a real failure mode that `startupProbe` specifically fixes.

## The core problem with `initialDelaySeconds` alone

`initialDelaySeconds` is a **fixed, static delay** — you set it once and it applies to every single pod startup, every time. This creates a direct tradeoff:

**If your app sometimes takes 20 seconds to start, sometimes 60 seconds** (real world — depends on database migrations, cache warming, JVM initialization, etc.), what do you set?

- **Set it to 20s** → fast-starting pods are fine, but slow-starting pods get killed by liveness probe before they're ready — because liveness starts checking at second 20, the app isn't up yet, `failureThreshold: 3` × `periodSeconds: 5` = 15 more seconds of checking before kill. App gets killed at ~35 seconds even though it just needed 60.
- **Set it to 60s** → slow-starting pods are fine, but now **every** pod wastes 60 seconds of dead waiting time even when the app was actually ready at second 10. Multiply by 50 pods in a rolling deploy — that's a lot of unnecessary delay.

There's no value that's simultaneously "safe for slow starts" and "fast for normal starts" when startup time is variable.

## What `startupProbe` actually does differently

`startupProbe` acts as a **gate** — while it's still checking, liveness and readiness probes are completely **disabled and not running at all**. The moment startupProbe succeeds once, it disables itself and hands control over to liveness and readiness.

```yaml
startupProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /healthz
    port: 8080
  failureThreshold: 30      # 30 × 10s = 300 seconds maximum startup window
  periodSeconds: 10
```

This gives you up to **300 seconds** of startup budget, but the moment the app is ready (even at second 8), the probe succeeds, the gate opens, and liveness/readiness kick in immediately — not after a fixed 300-second wait. Fast apps get fast handover; slow apps get their full time budget without being killed.

## Side by side — your exact YAML, improved

```yaml
containers:
  - name: http
    image: registry.k8s.io/e2e-test-images/agnhost:2.40
    args:
      - liveness

    # Gate: liveness + readiness are completely suspended
    # until this succeeds once, or until 30×10s=300s elapses
    startupProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /healthz
        port: 8080
      failureThreshold: 30   # max startup budget = 300 seconds
      periodSeconds: 10      # checks every 10s during startup
      # NO initialDelaySeconds needed here — startupProbe itself
      # IS the delay, but adaptive rather than fixed

    # Now runs only AFTER startupProbe has passed
    # No initialDelaySeconds needed here either — startup is already
    # confirmed done by the time liveness begins
    livenessProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /healthz
        port: 8080
      periodSeconds: 5
      failureThreshold: 3
      successThreshold: 1
      # initialDelaySeconds: 5  ← can remove this entirely

    # Same — readiness starts checking immediately post-startup
    readinessProbe:
      httpGet:
        path: /healthz
        port: 8080
      periodSeconds: 5
      failureThreshold: 3
      successThreshold: 1
      # initialDelaySeconds: 5  ← can remove this too

    ports:
      - containerPort: 8080
```

## The three scenarios compared

| Scenario | `initialDelaySeconds` only | `startupProbe` |
|---|---|---|
| App starts in 5s, delay set to 60s | Waits full 60s regardless — wastes 55s | StartupProbe succeeds at 5s → immediate handover |
| App starts in 90s, delay set to 60s | Liveness fires at 60s, kills pod at ~75s — app never makes it | StartupProbe keeps checking until 90s, succeeds, all good |
| App starts in 90s, delay set to 120s | Works but always wastes 30+s even on fast starts | Same as above — adaptive |
| App is stuck in infinite boot loop | Has to wait full `initialDelaySeconds` before liveness can detect it | StartupProbe catches it within `failureThreshold × periodSeconds` |

## One thing that surprises people — what happens when startupProbe fails entirely

If the startupProbe never succeeds within `failureThreshold × periodSeconds`, the container is **killed and restarted** according to the pod's `restartPolicy` — the same outcome as a liveness failure, just triggered during the startup window instead of runtime. So it still catches permanently broken containers, it just gives them their full startup budget before giving up.

## When you'd keep `initialDelaySeconds` anyway

There's still one valid use: pairing `initialDelaySeconds` with `startupProbe` itself, specifically to skip the very first few seconds of startup before even attempting the first startupProbe check — useful if the app's HTTP server takes a known fixed minimum time to even bind to its port and you'd just be burning failed checks until then. In practice, most teams skip `initialDelaySeconds` on `startupProbe` since a few wasted probe attempts at the start are harmless, but the option exists.

**Bottom line:** `initialDelaySeconds` is a blunt instrument that forces you to commit to a fixed delay upfront. `startupProbe` is an adaptive gate that gives your app as much time as it needs (up to your maximum budget), while keeping liveness/readiness probes completely out of the way until the app has actually proven it's started — then immediately hands over for ongoing health monitoring.
